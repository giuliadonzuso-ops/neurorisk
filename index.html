NeuroRisk Chart

An evidence-based risk calculator for dementia and Parkinson’s disease, built on the Lancet Commission 2024 attributable-fraction model, age- and sex-specific baselines and, when known, APOE genotype.

For education and discussion. Estimates are indicative and not validated for individual clinical decisions. Every parameter and assumption is listed under “Model & evidence”.

      No validated attributable-fraction model exists for Parkinson’s disease. Factors are combined as independent; smoking, caffeine and urate are left out on purpose because their inverse associations are confounded and not actionable.

      Biomarker layer

      Brain health services only

      The fourth domain of the European model, risk factors for neurodegenerative disease, means Alzheimer pathology measured with plasma p-tau217 or amyloid PET. It belongs to secondary prevention in people who ask for it, with structured counselling, so it is not scored here.

      • 20–40% lifetime risk of cognitive impairment in a 65-year-old man with positive amyloid and normal cognition Frisoni 2025
      • 13–37% of people seen in memory clinics have cognitive complaints without objective impairment, the target group of brain health services Frisoni 2025
      • p-tau217 blood tests make Alzheimer pathology measurable at scale; disclosure must separate being at risk from having a disease Frisoni 2025

      How the estimate is built

      The calculator chains three published layers. An age- and sex-specific baseline incidence gives the risk of an average person; a relative-risk multiplier moves that person up or down according to APOE genotype and the modifiable factors present; competing mortality turns hazards into cumulative risks.

      Effective RR of factor i RRᵢ* = 1 / (1 − k·(1 − cᵢ)·(RRᵢ − 1)/RRᵢ) cᵢ = communality (overlap with other factors, Lancet 2024, HUNT data) k = 1.138, set so that the combined population PAF equals 45.3% Modifiable multiplier M = Π RRᵢ* (factors present) / Π (1 + pᵢ·(RRᵢ* − 1)) APOE multiplier G = RR(genotype) / Σ freq·RR (1 if unknown) Individual hazard h(a) = h₀(a, sex) · G · M Cumulative risk ∫ h(a)·S(a) da, S = survival free of dementia and death

      Dividing by the population average means a person with an average exposure profile receives the baseline risk for their age and sex, so the baseline is not counted twice. The “modifiable share” shown for a person, 1 − 1/Π RRᵢ*, is the individual counterpart of the population attributable fraction.

      Age windows. Late-life factors (social isolation, air pollution, untreated vision loss) count only from age 65; early-life and midlife factors count at any age as a record of past exposure.

      Two combination methods. The default corrects each RR for overlap and divides by the population average. The alternative multiplies the raw Lancet RRs (the “Lancet RR composite”) and applies the product to the baseline. This is not a validated epidemiological method and can inflate estimates because factors are not independent; showing both lets the room see how much the choice matters.

      Structure and communication. Factors are grouped into the four domains of the European task force for brain health services: constitutional, brain reserve, vascular-metabolic and neurodegenerative (biomarkers). Results are given as absolute risks over 5 years, 10 years and lifetime with an icon array, as recommended for risk communication, together with what can still be changed.

      Main limitations

      Sleep disturbance is listed by the European model under brain reserve, but it is not one of the 14 Lancet factors, so it is recorded and not scored. Relative risks come from observational meta-analyses and are treated as causal, as the Lancet Commission does; the PAF is a theoretical ceiling. Midlife exposures are applied at any age, although their effect on late-life risk depends on timing. APOE effects are held constant with age, whereas they are strongest between 65 and 80. Baseline incidence values are approximate European figures from cohorts of the 1990s; age-specific incidence has since fallen by about 13% per decade, so absolute values may overestimate current risk. The Parkinson’s module uses independent factors and assumed exposure prevalences. None of this replaces validated scores such as CAIDE or LIBRA in clinical practice.

      Questions for the workshop

      1. InclusionWhich factors belong in the chart? Should sleep, diet and the Mediterranean pattern enter despite weaker RCT evidence?
      2. WeightingRelative risk, attributable fraction or strength of evidence: what should drive the weight of each factor?
      3. GeneticsShould APOE be offered, and with what counselling, when results change risk but not what a person can do?
      4. CommunicationHow do we show risk without false reassurance for the low-risk, or fatalism for ε4 homozygotes?
      5. One or two scoresA shared brain-health chart, or separate dementia and Parkinson’s scores given divergent factors such as smoking?
